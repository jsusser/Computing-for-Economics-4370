\section{Cleaned Data}

We now describe how the raw merged frame is converted into the feature matrix \(X\) and label vector \(y\).

We keep a single binary label, \texttt{dead}, which equals one if the individual is recorded as dead at the 2019 follow up and zero otherwise. Rows are dropped only when this label is missing. Predictors are allowed to be missing and are imputed as described below.

Numeric features such as age and income to poverty ratio (PIR) are imputed using the within sample median and accompanied by explicit missing value flags. Categorical features such as sex and smoker status are imputed by adding an \texttt{Unknown} level when needed. Age and PIR are then standardized to mean zero and variance one. We also create the engineered terms
\(\texttt{age\_sq} = \texttt{age}^2\) and \(\texttt{log\_income} = \log(1 + \text{PIR})\).

\section{Transformations and Smoking Coding}

This section explains the logic of the main transformations and the construction of the smoker categories. The goal is to follow NHANES style coding while keeping the design easy to interpret in linear models.

\subsection*{Label rule}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The label is \(\texttt{dead}\in\{0,1\}\).
  \item We drop rows only when \texttt{dead} is missing.
  \item All predictors are kept whenever possible and imputed when necessary.
\end{itemize}

This label first strategy preserves sample size and keeps the target definition simple.

\subsection*{Smoking variables}

The smoking module in NHANES style data uses a set of questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{SMQ020}: indicator for having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in life.
  \item \texttt{SMQ040}: current smoking frequency, with categories such as every day, some days, or not at all.
  \item \texttt{SMQ060}: follow up question on smoking history for some respondents, typically used to clarify former use.
\end{itemize}

We collapse these into three mutually exclusive categories:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Never}: \texttt{SMQ020} indicates fewer than 100 lifetime cigarettes.
  \item \textbf{Former}: \texttt{SMQ020} indicates at least 100 lifetime cigarettes and \texttt{SMQ040} reports no current smoking.
  \item \textbf{Current}: \texttt{SMQ020} indicates at least 100 lifetime cigarettes and \texttt{SMQ040} reports current smoking on every day or some days.
\end{itemize}

These three levels form the categorical variable \texttt{smoker}. In the models we encode \texttt{smoker} using two dummy variables, \texttt{smokerFormer} and \texttt{smokerNever}, and treat Current smokers as the omitted reference group. We also store an ordinal code \texttt{smoker\_code} equal to 1, 2, or 3 for Never, Former, and Current. This ordinal code is never used directly in the outcome models but can be helpful in auxiliary summaries or plots.

\subsection*{Other transformations}

The remaining main transformations are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Age and income standardization}: both age and PIR are centered and scaled so that one unit corresponds to one sample standard deviation. This makes coefficients easier to compare.
  \item \textbf{Squared age \(\texttt{age\_sq}\)}: allows the model to capture curvature in the effect of age on mortality risk.
  \item \textbf{Log income \(\texttt{log\_income}\)}: reduces skewness in the PIR distribution and approximates diminishing marginal returns of income for mortality risk.
  \item \textbf{Missing flags}: for each imputed variable we add a binary missingness indicator. This preserves the information that a value was originally missing which can sometimes be predictive in its own right.
\end{itemize}

\section{Models}

We estimate three models that all treat \texttt{dead} as a numeric outcome between zero and one:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A linear probability model estimated with ordinary least squares and HC1 robust standard errors.
  \item A LASSO regression with cross validated penalty parameter.
  \item A small feed forward neural network estimated by minimizing mean squared error.
\end{enumerate}

Throughout, \(\hat y_i\) denotes the predicted mortality risk for individual \(i\).

\subsection*{Model 1: OLS linear probability model}

The baseline model is

\[
y_i = \beta_0
    + \beta_1 \cdot \mathbb{1}\{\texttt{smoker}=\text{Former}\}_i
    + \beta_2 \cdot \mathbb{1}\{\texttt{smoker}=\text{Never}\}_i
    + \beta_3 \texttt{age}_i
    + \beta_4 \mathbb{1}\{\texttt{sex}=\text{male}\}_i
    + \beta_5 \texttt{income}_i
    + \varepsilon_i.
\]

Here age and income are standardized. The error term \(\varepsilon_i\) is allowed to be heteroskedastic, which is unavoidable with a binary outcome. We therefore report HC1 robust standard errors.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Coefficients and robust standard errors.}
\par
\input{tables/smoking_ols_table.tex}

The coefficient patterns are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The intercept implies a baseline mortality probability of roughly 7.5 percent for a Current smoker with average age and income and female sex.
  \item A one standard deviation increase in age is associated with about 8.9 percentage points higher mortality risk. This is by far the largest effect in magnitude.
  \item A one standard deviation increase in standardized income is associated with about 2.7 percentage points lower mortality risk. Higher income is protective.
  \item Male sex is associated with roughly 1.3 percentage points higher risk relative to female sex, conditional on age, income, and smoking.
  \item Former smokers have about 1.6 percentage points higher estimated risk than otherwise similar Current smokers. Never smokers have about 1.4 percentage points lower estimated risk than Current smokers. The spread between Former and Never smokers is therefore around 3 percentage points.
\end{itemize}

Given the low overall death rate, a three percentage point spread across smoking categories is meaningful but still smaller than the effect of age.

\subsection*{Model 2: LASSO regression}

The LASSO solves

\[
\hat\beta(\lambda)
=
\arg\min_{\beta}
\left\{
  \frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^\top \beta)^2
  + \lambda \sum_{j} |\beta_j|
\right\},
\]

where \(\lambda \ge 0\) is a tuning parameter that controls the amount of shrinkage and variable selection. We use cross validation to choose two reference values: \(\lambda_{\min}\), which minimizes the cross validated mean squared error, and \(\lambda_{1\text{se}}\), which is the largest value whose error is within one standard error of that minimum.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Nonzero coefficients at \(\lambda_{\min}\).}
\par
\input{tables/lasso_coefficients.tex}

At \(\lambda_{\min}\) the LASSO keeps the same core predictors as the OLS model. The magnitudes of the coefficients are very close to the OLS values, which suggests that the OLS estimates are not overly sensitive to regularization.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Coefficient comparison across estimators.}
\par
\input{tables/coef_comparison.tex}

The comparison table shows two key patterns.

\begin{itemize}
  \item At \(\lambda_{\min}\), the LASSO coefficients essentially replicate OLS, with very small shrinkage in absolute value.
  \item At \(\lambda_{1\text{se}}\), only the intercept and the age coefficient remain nonzero. This more conservative choice of \(\lambda\) therefore identifies age as the only predictor that is reliably useful across folds, while income, sex, and smoking effects are shrunk to zero.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, these results say that smoking, sex, and income add some predictive power at the cross validated optimum, but age is the dominant driver of mortality risk in this simple specification.

\subsection*{Model 3: Neural network}

The third model is a small feed forward neural network with two hidden layers. In vector form the prediction is

\[
\hat y_i
=
f_{\text{out}}\!\left(
  W_2
  f_{\text{hid}}\!\left(
    W_1 x_i + b_1
  \right)
  + b_2
\right),
\]

where \(f_{\text{hid}}\) is a logistic activation applied elementwise, \(f_{\text{out}}\) is linear, \(x_i\) is the input feature vector, and \(W_1, W_2, b_1, b_2\) are weights and biases learned from the data. The network uses two hidden layers with 8 and 4 units. Inputs include standardized age and income and engineered terms but exclude race and education dummies, in order to keep the architecture small.

The network is trained by minimizing mean squared error using the \texttt{neuralnet} package and an 80/20 validation split. The table below reports the resulting in sample metrics.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Neural network in sample fit.}
\par
\input{tables/nn_metrics.tex}

The reported mean squared error is essentially zero and the reported \(R^2\) is essentially one. Such perfect in sample fit is implausible for a noisy binary outcome and strongly suggests overfitting or leakage of the label into the inputs or training procedure. Because of this, we treat the neural network results as a diagnostic about what can go wrong with overly flexible models rather than as evidence that mortality risk can be predicted almost perfectly.

\section{Diagnostics}

We now discuss standard diagnostics for the linear and LASSO models and comment on what they say about model adequacy.

\subsection*{Multicollinearity}

We begin with variance inflation factors (VIF) for the OLS specification.

\paragraph{Variance inflation factors.}
\verbatiminput{artifacts/vif.txt}

All VIF values are very close to one. This indicates that multicollinearity is negligible among the main regressors. In particular, the age, income, sex, and smoking variables are not so highly correlated that they would destabilize the coefficient estimates.

\subsection*{Model 1: OLS residual diagnostics}

For the OLS linear probability model we examine residuals versus fitted values, a normal Q-Q plot, and predicted risk by smoking status. Figures are placed immediately below their captions for clarity.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/residuals_vs_fitted.png}
  \caption{OLS residuals versus fitted values}
\end{figure}

In a linear probability model with a binary outcome, residual variance must depend on the fitted value \( \hat y_i \) because \(\operatorname{Var}(y_i \mid x_i) = \hat y_i(1 - \hat y_i)\). A residuals versus fitted plot therefore inevitably shows banding and nonconstant spread. This does not contradict the use of the model, but it means that homoskedasticity fails by construction. That is why we rely on HC1 robust standard errors rather than classical homoskedastic formulas.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/qq_plot.png}
  \caption{OLS normal Q-Q plot of residuals}
\end{figure}

The normal Q-Q plot compares the empirical distribution of residuals to a normal reference line. With a binary outcome, residuals are far from exactly normal and the plot deviates from the straight line, especially in the tails. This is expected and does not invalidate inference, because large sample theory for OLS only requires approximate normality of the \emph{coefficient estimates}, not of the raw residuals. Robust standard errors further reduce sensitivity to non normality.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/predicted_risk_by_smoker.png}
  \caption{OLS predicted mortality risk by smoking status}
\end{figure}

The predicted risk by smoking status figure averages the model based risk index within each of the three smoking categories. It shows that Former smokers have the highest predicted mortality risk, Never smokers have the lowest, and Current smokers lie in between. This pattern matches the coefficient signs in the OLS table and is consistent with a story in which Former smokers tend to be older and may have accumulated more damage before quitting, while Never smokers are healthier on average. The differences are around three percentage points, which is meaningful relative to a baseline death rate of 7.6 percent.

\subsection*{Model 2: LASSO diagnostics}

For the LASSO model we examine residuals versus fitted values constructed using the coefficients at \(\lambda_{\min}\), a normal Q-Q plot of those residuals, and the cross validation error curve as a function of \(\lambda\).

\begin{figure}[H]

  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/lasso_residuals_vs_fitted.png}

The LASSO residuals have the same basic structure as the OLS residuals, since the functional form is linear and the outcome is still binary. Heteroskedasticity remains inherent in the data generating process, and robust methods are again appropriate if we were to attach standard errors to the post LASSO coefficients.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/lasso_qq_plot.png}
  \caption{LASSO normal Q-Q plot of residuals at \(\lambda_{\min}\)}

The Q-Q plot for the LASSO residuals again shows deviations from normality that are similar to those in the OLS case. This is expected for a binary dependent variable and reinforces that Gaussian error assumptions are not literally true here.

\begin{figure}[H]

  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/lasso_cv_plot.png}

\end{figure}

The cross validation plot shows how out of sample mean squared error changes with the penalty parameter. The minimum identifies \(\lambda_{\min}\), while the horizontal band at one standard error above the minimum determines \(\lambda_{1\text{se}}\). The fact that these two values are fairly close and lead to similar fitted values suggests that there is no dramatic instability in model selection.

\section{Model comparison}

We summarize overall fit across the three models using common error metrics.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Global error metrics.}
\par
\input{tables/model_metrics.tex}

Several points stand out.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The OLS and LASSO models have very similar mean squared error, root mean squared error, mean absolute error, and \(R^2\). Differences are only in the third decimal place. This confirms that LASSO regularization does not produce a practically different fit in this low dimensional setting.
  \item The \(R^2\) values around 0.13 for the linear models are modest but reasonable for a binary outcome with few predictors. They indicate that age, income, sex, and smoking explain some, but far from all, of the variation in mortality status.
  \item The neural network shows nearly zero error and an \(R^2\) of essentially one. As discussed earlier, this is not believable as a measure of real world predictive power. It reflects overfitting to the training data or label leakage rather than genuine signal extraction.
\end{itemize}

From a substantive point of view, the linear models are preferable, since they are transparent, stable under regularization, and provide interpretable coefficients.

\section{Takeaways}

Putting all pieces together, the cleaned simulated data behave in a way that is consistent with basic epidemiological intuition.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Mortality between the initial exam and 2019 is relatively rare, with about 7.6 percent of adults recorded as dead.
  \item Age is the dominant predictor of mortality risk. A one standard deviation increase in age raises predicted risk by nearly nine percentage points in the linear models.
  \item Higher income is associated with lower mortality risk, while male sex is associated with slightly higher risk.
  \item Smoking status matters, but its effect is smaller than the effect of age. Former smokers have the highest predicted risk, Never smokers the lowest, and Current smokers lie in between. This pattern likely reflects the joint influence of age, intensity of smoking, and selection into quitting.
  \item Linear and LASSO models provide very similar predictions and both fit the data about as well as can be expected given the limited set of predictors.
  \item The neural network highlights the danger of overfitting when flexible models are used on modest sized datasets without strict separation of training and evaluation data.
  \item Bootstrap based Monte Carlo experiments confirm that age is included in essentially every LASSO fit and that the core set of covariates is selected with high frequency, which supports the robustness of the main findings.
\end{itemize}

Overall, the project delivers a coherent cleaned dataset, transparent models, and diagnostics that make clear where the linear probability framework is adequate and where it relies on robust methods to compensate for heteroskedastic and non normal residuals.
